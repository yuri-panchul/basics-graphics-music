After 6.111: How 111 Universities Score on 49 Topics Beyond the First FPGA Course

A course like MIT 6.111 (now 6.205) is where many engineers first meet Verilog and an FPGA board. For a lot of universities it is also where hardware teaching ends: gates, state machines, a UART or SPI interface, perhaps a five-stage MIPS or RISC-V pipeline. Chip companies then hire these graduates to build data pipelines that have nothing to do with a CPU, to verify them, and to make them meet timing.

The talk “Workforce Development for the Electronic Industry” at EDA Connect 2025 named three gaps that show up in job interviews (slides 14–17): microarchitecture (pipelines, FIFOs, flow control), design verification (checking against a transaction-level model, corner cases) and physical design (static timing analysis, negative slack, clock gating). A new report turns these gaps into a 49-item checklist and scores 111 universities and programs against it.

The checklist

  • Microarchitecture with one clock: data pipelining, FIFOs, pipelines with FIFOs for flow control, valid/ready, credit-based flow control, systolic arrays, arbiters, reorder buffers, AXI.
  • Several clocks, memories and clock gating: SRAM banking, linked-list controllers, clock-domain crossing, asynchronous FIFOs, clock gating with enables and with latch-based gaters.
  • Verification: self-checking testbenches, checking against transaction-level models, bus functional models, monitors and scoreboards, constrained random, functional coverage, SVA assertions in simulation, formal verification, UVM, emulators, checking a CPU against an instruction-set simulator.
  • Timing and physical design: Tiny Tapeout, open-source flows (OpenROAD, LibreLane), commercial tools, labs with STA reports, power analysis and floorplanning.
  • Processor labs in RTL: in-order and out-of-order pipelines, multithreading, caches, multicore clusters, coherent caches, branch predictors.
  • Application projects: video, graphics, networking, filters, FFT, audio, ML accelerators.
  • EDA algorithms: students writing their own simulators, synthesis or place-and-route tools.

The ranking

The universities from the original report (California, Russia, Belarus, Ukraine and the other former Soviet republics) are joined by the Digital Circuit Synthesis School and twenty universities and programs elsewhere: MIT, Princeton, Michigan, NC State, Portland State, Georgia Tech, ETH Zürich, EPFL, Cambridge, UCL, Tsinghua, China's One Student One Chip, NYCU, National Taiwan University, the University of Tokyo, Science Tokyo, IIT Bombay, IIT Madras, KAIST and POSTECH.

What stands out

  • Only MIT covers more than half the list. The leaders combine several courses: an FPGA lab, then advanced design or verification, then an ASIC flow.
  • MIET is the only former Soviet university near the top. Its open FPGA practicum covers pipelines, systolic arrays, AXI-Stream with credit-based flow control and asynchronous FIFOs, and a verification elective adds constrained random, coverage and SVA.
  • The Synthesis School covers more microarchitecture items (8 of 9) than any university. Its SystemVerilog homework drills pipelines, FIFOs, valid/ready flow control, arbiters and putting results back in order.
  • Clock-domain crossing is documented at 6 entries, clock gating at 2. Four items appear nowhere: linked-list controllers, latch-based clock gaters, coherence-free clusters of cores, and student-written RTL simulators.
  • 72 of the 111 entries have no documented point beyond the introductory course. Many of them may teach more than their public pages show.

What universities can do

  1. Teach pipelines that aren't CPUs, with FIFOs, valid/ready and credit-based flow control, in the first or second course. Open exercise sets make this cheap.
  2. Add a verification course, or a verification half to the FPGA course: self-checking against a model, constrained random, functional coverage, assertions. cocotb and free simulators are enough to start.
  3. Give students an ASIC flow. Open-source tools (Yosys, OpenROAD, LibreLane, OpenSTA), open PDKs (SkyWater 130, GF180, IHP 130) and shuttles such as Tiny Tapeout make timing reports, slack and clock gating hands-on, as ETH Zürich and One Student One Chip show.
  4. Publish syllabi and lab handouts. Most of the zeros in this report come from courses whose content isn't public.

How the points were counted

One point per item when a public course page, catalog, syllabus, lab manual, slide deck or student repository shows it in the introductory FPGA course or a related course. A missing point means no public evidence was found, not that the topic isn't taught. Every point in the report links to its source.
