After the first FPGA course, then what? Many universities teach one digital design course: gates, state machines, a UART or an SPI interface, maybe a five-stage MIPS pipeline. Then their graduates interview at chip companies and get asked about FIFOs inside data pipelines, valid/ready and credit-based flow control, constrained-random testbenches, functional coverage, SVA, timing reports and negative slack. A new report scores 111 universities and programs on 49 such topics. The leaders: MIT (25 of 49), UC Berkeley (19), Michigan (17), MIET and NYCU (16 each), China's One Student One Chip (15), Portland State (13), NC State (12), the Digital Circuit Synthesis School (11) and MIPT (10). Two things stood out for the former Soviet Union. First, MIET in Zelenograd is the only university there near the top. Its open FPGA practicum covers pipelines, systolic arrays, AXI-Stream with credit-based flow control and asynchronous FIFOs, and a verification elective adds constrained-random tests, coverage and SVA. Second, physical design is the weakest spot. Only KSE in Kyiv (Cadence tools, Tiny Tapeout), the Synopsys-linked programs in Armenia and at MIET, and the Synthesis School's lecture on the open ASIC flow reach it. ETH Zürich and China's One Student One Chip show that open-source tools and open PDKs are enough to teach timing reports, slack and a full flow to tapeout. 72 of the 111 entries have no documented point beyond the introductory course, often because the syllabi aren't public. If your university teaches any of these topics and the report misses it, send a link to the syllabus or lab handouts.
