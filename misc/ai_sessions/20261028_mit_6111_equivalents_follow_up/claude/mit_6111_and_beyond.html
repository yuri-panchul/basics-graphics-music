6.111 Counterparts and Beyond

The course at each university that comes closest to MIT 6.111, the FPGA lab where students learn Verilog and then build a large project of their own, with a link to each course page. A follow-up chapter, After 6.111, scores these universities, the Synthesis School and 20 more universities and programs on 49 topics that a single FPGA course rarely reaches: flow control, clock-domain crossing, modern verification, timing and physical design.

The reference course

6.2056.111

Digital Systems Laboratory I

MIT renumbered 6.111 (Introductory Digital Systems Laboratory) as 6.205 in its new numbering.

  • Digital design on FPGAs in Verilog, simulated with iVerilog and built with AMD Vivado.
  • Each student borrows a Real Digital Urbana board for the term.
  • The first half is lectures and about eight weekly labs and exercises (48% of the grade). The second half is a large final project (52%). There are no exams.
  • 12 units (1-5-6). The prerequisite is 6.191, formerly 6.004.

How the regions compare

What the searches show about each region. It reflects what universities publish online, which varies a lot from region to region, as well as what they teach.

California

8 of 17 close

FPGA labs are part of the first or second digital design course at nearly every school checked, from Berkeley and Stanford to the Cal State campuses. The closest equivalents pair HDL labs with a large project of the student's choice: Berkeley 151LB, Harvey Mudd E155 (labs and project 45% each), Caltech 119, USC 354L and UC Santa Cruz 125. Catalogs are public and current but seldom name a board. Those named are Digilent boards (Basys 3, Nexys4, PYNQ) and Terasic boards (DE0-CV, DE1, DE10-Lite, DE2-115), plus Berkeley's Zynq UltraScale+ AUP-ZU3 and Harvey Mudd's own iCE40 board.

Russia

7 of 28 close

The widest spread of FPGA teaching, with the most current materials in Moscow: MIET's open RISC-V course book, MIPT's pipelined RISC-V course, MPEI's lab manual with Icarus Verilog and Cyclone IV, and MIEM's SoC courses. Regional universities such as those in Voronezh, Novosibirsk, Tomsk and Nizhny Novgorod have FPGA courses whose public syllabi mostly date from 2016–2022, often in VHDL on older boards (Spartan-3, Cyclone II); several include a course project built on the board. The boards named are mostly Terasic DE-series and Digilent Nexys, alongside cheaper Chinese and Russian boards (OMDAZZ at MPEI, Marsohod2 and LESO2 at SibSUTIS) and old Xilinx Starter Kits. YADRO's free Synthesis School, at 26 sites, is the closest thing to a nationwide 6.111. DonNTU, in the Russian-controlled part of Ukraine's Donetsk region, describes FPGA work but publishes no syllabus.

Belarus

1 of 5 close

Concentrated in two places: BSUIR in Minsk, with its YADRO lab and a two-semester FPGA course, and Gomel, with two Synthesis School clusters. BNTU has a 2013 VHDL lab practicum in Xilinx ISE that ends with a PicoBlaze system. The other universities publish little at course level, and the only board named is a Basys 3 in a BNTU conference paper.

Ukraine

1 of 7 close

Detailed public syllabi at KPI, KNU and Odesa Polytechnic show FPGA labs on Altera-era hardware in Quartus II (Terasic DE boards, ACEX boards, a DIGIASIC stand), built around DSP and interfaces rather than open-ended projects. KSE's new master's program, run with ETH Zürich on Zybo Z7 boards, is the most current and the closest match.

Other former Soviet republics

1 of 33 close, 19 not found

The Baltic states publish current course records in ECTS, often in English as well: the University of Latvia's DIP course builds pipelined CPUs and ends in projects, and TalTech and RTU teach SystemVerilog or VHDL with mini-projects or individual projects. Elsewhere there are one to three universities with a course per country in Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Armenia, Azerbaijan, Moldova and Georgia, and none were found in Uzbekistan, Tajikistan or Turkmenistan. Armenia's chip-design teaching runs mainly through Synopsys and ASIC tools rather than FPGAs; its FPGA activity shows up in events such as the 2025 EDA Connect hackathon on Gowin boards, and in Kyrgyzstan in AUCA's 2022 Siemens EDA seminar. Boards are named only at the University of Latvia (Spartan-3E, Anvyl, PYNQ-Z1), TalTech (Nexys 4 DDR) and ADA University (ALINX AX4010).

Across all regions

  • The strongest matches usually have an industry or university partner behind them: YADRO in Russia and Belarus, ETH Zürich at KSE, Synopsys in Armenia. The other strong ones are long-running courses with open materials, like MIET's and Berkeley's.
  • Building a processor, usually RISC-V, is the typical capstone of the strongest courses: MIET, MIPT, SibSUTIS, the University of Latvia, Berkeley and Harvey Mudd's E85.
  • Two board families dominate everywhere: Terasic boards with Intel (Altera) FPGAs and Digilent boards with AMD (Xilinx) FPGAs. Cheaper boards appear too: Chinese Cyclone IV boards such as OMDAZZ at MPEI, Russian Marsohod and LESO boards at SibSUTIS, and Gowin Tang boards on the Synthesis School's list. Among these universities only Harvey Mudd teaches on Lattice. Most sources don't name a board at all, so the board column is often empty.
  • Public evidence thins out sharply away from Moscow, St. Petersburg and the Baltic capitals, so “Not found” in Central Asia and the Caucasus says more about what's online than about what's taught.

Follow-up chapter

After 6.111: One FPGA Lab Is Not a Curriculum

A course like 6.111 is a good start, but it is only a start. At many universities, and in some whole countries, hardware teaching ends with one class that runs from gates to finite state machines and serial interfaces such as SPI. When that class covers pipelining at all, it is the classic five-stage MIPS or RISC-V pipeline.

Graduates who join chip companies soon meet designs whose pipelines have nothing to do with a CPU: packet processing in networking chips, video processing blocks, fixed-function units in GPUs. Data in these designs moves through pipelines and FIFOs that must stall and resume without losing a word. Most graduates know what a FIFO is but not how to use one together with a pipeline, and few can explain credit-based flow control in a job interview.

Verification is the second gap. Many graduates write testbenches in the style of the early 1990s. They have not checked a design against a transaction-level model, written constrained-random stimulus or measured functional coverage in SystemVerilog. Very few have used formal verification or concurrent assertions, although their AI-written résumés often list them. Engineers who can't verify corner cases have to keep their microarchitecture simple.

The third gap separates FPGA labs from ASIC design. Some universities have no access to Synopsys Fusion Compiler or Cadence Genus and Innovus, and no expertise in open-source flows such as OpenROAD and LibreLane. Their students don't develop an intuition for static timing analysis, don't know how to fix negative slack, and are often confused about power-saving techniques such as clock gating.

These three gaps (microarchitecture, design verification and physical design) are the subject of slides 14–17 of a talk on workforce development for the electronics industry given at EDA Connect in Yerevan in March 2025. This chapter turns them into a 49-item checklist and scores every university and program in this report against it, adding 20 universities and programs from other US states, Europe and Asia for comparison.

How the points are counted

  • One point per item, when a public source shows it in the university's introductory FPGA course or in a related course: advanced digital design, computer architecture, verification, VLSI or VLSI CAD. Graduate courses count. The Synthesis School and China's One Student One Chip program are scored as entries of their own, since students take them alongside their degrees.
  • Sources are course pages, catalogs, syllabi, lab manuals and slides. Where a university publishes little, I used public repositories in which students post their coursework (Michigan, NYCU, National Taiwan University, the University of Tokyo, IIT Bombay, IIT Madras). They show what one team built in a given year.
  • Item 1.1 counts data pipelining outside a CPU; a processor pipeline counts as 5.1. Area 5 counts only labs or projects in which students build or change an RTL processor, and area 7 only work in which they write or change EDA software. Lecture topics count in areas 1–3.
  • The SystemVerilog items also count when a course teaches the same technique in another framework, such as cocotb. A course that teaches UVM earns item 3.9 and also items 3.2–3.5, because drivers, monitors, scoreboards, transaction-level checking and constrained-random sequences are its standard parts; a course can earn 3.2–3.5 without UVM, with plain SystemVerilog classes as in Chris Spear's textbook “SystemVerilog for Verification”. Item 3.7 counts SVA concurrent assertions used in simulation, and item 3.8 counts formal verification in JasperGold, VC Formal or an open-source equivalent.
  • Item 4.4 needs ASIC timing reports; FPGA timing reports from Vivado or Quartus don't count. Item 4.3 needs commercial tools named in the source.
  • A missing point means I found no public evidence, not that the topic isn't taught. Pages at Princeton and UCL, Michigan's course site and Stanford's ExploreCourses refused automated downloads, and MIT's 6.205 pages, NYCU's lecture slides and Cambridge's engineering pages need a login. Their scores are lower bounds, as are the scores of universities whose syllabi sit in Moodle, which covers most of the former Soviet Union.
  • The sources were checked on 28 October 2026. Each dot in the tables links to its source, and the list at the end of the chapter gives every source for every university.

What the scores show

  • Only MIT covers more than half the list, with 25 of 49. It is followed by UC Berkeley (19), Michigan (17), MIET and NYCU (16 each) and China's One Student One Chip program (15).
  • The leaders combine several courses: an FPGA lab, an advanced digital or verification course and an ASIC course. MIT adds 6.S965 (cocotb monitors, scoreboards and coverage, skid buffers, AXI) and 6.1920 (pipelines, caches and coherence in Bluespec) to 6.205. Teams in Michigan's EECS 470 build out-of-order superscalar cores. NYCU's ICLAB packs asynchronous FIFOs checked in JasperGold, constrained-random testbenches, low-power design and place and route into one semester. Berkeley's EECS 151 runs FPGA and ASIC labs side by side.
  • MIET is the one former Soviet university at that level. Its FPGA practicum covers pipelines and systolic arrays, AXI-Stream with credit-based flow control and asynchronous FIFOs, and a verification elective adds constrained-random tests, coverage, SVA assertions in simulation and APB/AXI verification IP. The Synthesis School covers 8 of the 9 microarchitecture items, more than any university, and is the only entry besides MIET with credit-based flow control. MIPT adds a pipelined FPU, asynchronous FIFOs and, from 2026, systolic arrays and AXI.
  • Verification beyond simple testbenches shows up where a university has a dedicated course: NC State, Portland State, UC Berkeley, MIT's 6.S965, NYCU and MIET's elective. Formal verification is documented at 8 entries, but SVA assertions in simulation only at Berkeley, Portland State and MIET, and UVM only at Berkeley, MIT, NC State and Portland State. MIET's elective builds drivers, monitors and checkers from plain SystemVerilog classes, without UVM. Only One Student One Chip documents checking a processor against an instruction-set simulator.
  • Physical design is rare. In the former Soviet Union only KSE (Cadence tools, Tiny Tapeout), the Synopsys-linked programs in Armenia and at MIET, and the Synthesis School's lecture on the open ASIC flow reach area 4. ETH Zürich and One Student One Chip show that a full flow, up to tapeout, is possible with open-source tools and open PDKs.
  • Area 2 is the weakest: clock-domain crossing appears at 6 entries and clock gating at 2. Four items appear nowhere: linked-list controllers, latch-based clock gaters, a cluster of cores without coherence, and student-written RTL simulators. Another 9 appear at only one entry, among them SRAM banking, hardware multithreading and coherent caches.
  • 72 of the 111 entries have no documented point. For most of them, mainly in the former Soviet Union and among smaller California universities, the only public course is the introductory one.
