Why $79

It sits below the hands-on AI workshops. The nearest paid comparison, Frontier Tower's “Claude Code for Beginners” in San Francisco, charges $149 to $199 for 4.5 hours, $33/h to $44/h [5]. But it seats 12, and each seat comes with a laptop and a 7-day API key. Your session is a lecture with live demos: nobody gets a board or a machine, so it should cost less per hour. $79 is $26/h.

It sits well above Hacker Dojo's usual events, and it should. The Dojo's membership already includes free talks, hackathons and workshops [1], its own AI workshop was free [2], and your Verilog Meetups there listed no price [3]. A paid ticket for a first-of-its-kind session is reasonable. The early-bird, student and member tiers keep the people who came to the free meetups.

Vendor FPGA training no longer sets a high mark, and it doesn't teach this. Altera's public classes cost $695 per student per day in 2012 [8] and are free now [9]. AMD lists its 21-hour Vivado course at $299 [6]. None of them covers four vendors in one infrastructure, or using AI agents to write and check a board support package. The market that prices that content is AI workshops, not vendor courses.

The content is hard to get elsewhere. Four FPGA families in one session, a working open-source repository the attendees can keep using, and real case studies: board ports done by Claude and by Codex, compared side by side.

Paying attendees show up. A ticket of $79 filters casual sign-ups. With four boards on one table, a known head count lets you plan the room, the camera and the seats.

Above about $100 the session would compete with the hands-on workshops, which include hardware, on equal terms. Start at $79; if the first session fills a week ahead, charge $99 for the next one.

What you keep per ticket

Hacker Dojo lists its events on Luma. Luma's free plan takes 5 % on paid tickets, and Stripe takes 2.9 % plus 30 cents [10]. Eventbrite, with the fees absorbed by you rather than added for the buyer, takes 3.7 % plus $1.79 a ticket, plus 2.9 % for payment processing [11]. Hands-on: a laptop and a board for each person

In this variant you bring a laptop for each person, with all four toolchains installed, and one board per person from one of the four families. Attendees pick the family when they buy the ticket; the demos still show all four. The class is 10 or 15 people.

Price: $149 early bird, $179 standard, $99 student. The standard seat sits between Frontier Tower's $149 and $199 seats [5], but for 3 hours instead of 4.5: $60/h, between Frontier's $33/h to $44/h and Altera's 2012 classroom rate of $82/h [8]. In return each person gets a real FPGA board and a laptop where nothing needs installing, in a class of 10 or 15 rather than a lecture hall.

Bought for one class, the equipment costs more than the tickets bring. Spread over 10 classes, a 10-seat class leaves $1,007, against $1,154 for a 20-person lecture, and a 15-seat class leaves $1,523. For 15 seats that is $25/h of work, counting 55 hours of preparation (the lecture's 40 plus 15 to write the labs and set up the laptops) and 6 hours on the day.

Choose 15 seats if you can find a helper. The equipment costs more ($6,138 against $4,079), but the tickets bring more too, and the fixed hours stay the same. With four toolchains in one room, 15 people need a helper; you can run 10 alone.

What changes in the session. 100 slides and labs don't fit in 3 hours: keep about 40 slides for the first hour and give two hours to the labs, or make the session 4 hours. Attendees sign in to their own Claude and ChatGPT accounts on your laptops and sign out at the end, or you hand out temporary API keys, as Frontier Tower does; measure their cost in a dry run. Bring a spare laptop and a spare board of each family.

The private-session price

AMD's training FAQ says private classes are usually priced at the cost of 10 students [7]; at $79 that is $790. Altera's 2012 price for training at the customer's site was $4,500 a day for up to 16 students [8], about $2,250 for half a day. $2,000 for a 3-hour session for a team of up to 16, with the boards brought to the company, sits in that range and covers the preparation within a few sessions.

Before you announce it

  • Ask Hacker Dojo whether a member may charge for an event, whether the Dojo takes a room fee or a share, whether members must be admitted free, and how many people the room at 3200 Patrick Henry Dr seats. Its site says only that members can request event space [1].
  • Cap the seats at what can see the boards: about 30, with a camera on the boards and the image on the projector.
  • Record each Claude and Codex demo in advance, in case the network or an AI service is slow on the day.
  • Offer a full refund until 48 hours before, and keep a waiting list.
  • Promise the take-aways in the announcement: the slides, the BGM repository, and the reports in misc/ai_sessions.
